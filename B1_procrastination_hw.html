Why Do We Keep Waiting?
“I’ll start on Monday.”
“I’ll do it when I have more time.”
“I’ll begin when I feel ready.”

Do these sentences sound familiar? Most of us postpone things we know are important — exercising, learning something new, having a difficult conversation, or even starting to live the life we really want. This habit is called procrastination, and researchers say it affects around 20% of adults regularly.

It’s not about laziness

Psychologists say procrastination is not really about being lazy. It is usually about feelings. We put things off because we are afraid of failure, because a task feels too big, or because we prefer the comfort of staying exactly where we are. Our brains find it easier to choose something enjoyable right now — a TV series, another scroll through social media — than something difficult that might pay off later.

There is also a trap called the “someday” mentality: the belief that the right time will come. “Someday I will travel.” “Someday I will start that project.” “Someday I will tell that person how I feel.” But research is clear: someday almost never arrives on its own. The right moment is not found — it is created.

What we regret most

A nurse called Bronnie Ware worked with people at the very end of their lives and asked them what they regretted most. The number-one answer was not “I wish I’d worked harder.” It was:

“I wish I’d had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me.”
— Bronnie Ware, The Top Five Regrets of the Dying

In other words, people’s biggest regrets were not the mistakes they made — they were the things they never tried.

The good news

You do not need to change everything at once. Research on habits shows that small actions build momentum — the energy that keeps you moving forward. One phone call. One hour of practice. One honest conversation. The secret is not waiting until you feel ready. It is starting before you do.
