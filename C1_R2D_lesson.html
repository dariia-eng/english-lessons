Definitions
aTo discuss a problem intensively until a solution is reached
bTo raise a sensitive or difficult topic for the first time
cTo interrupt someone while they are still talking
dTo keep talking about the same thing at length, often boringly
eLight, friendly small talk about nothing important
fTo suddenly stop talking, usually from nerves or discomfort
gTo consult someone to reach a shared view or decision
hTo lose focus and start talking about something unrelated
iA serious, personal conversation between two people
jPlayful, friendly teasing between people who know each other well
kTo say something again to stress it or make sure it is understood
lTo talk about work in a social situation where it is out of place

Study how each item is used grammatically, where the main stress falls, and whether it's appropriate in formal or informal contexts. The underlined syllable carries primary stress.
ItemGrammatical patternStressRegister & typical context
butt in butt in (on sb/sth) · "Can I just butt in?" BUTT IN Informal — social / meetings
heart-to-heart have a heart-to-heart (with sb) · a h-t-h talk/chat HEART-to-HEART Neutral — close relationships
chit-chat have a chit-chat · chit-chat about sth (v) CHIT-chat Informal — social settings
reiterate reiterate (that…) · reiterate a point / message re·IT·er·ate Formal — meetings, writing, speeches

thrash it out thrash sth out (with sb) · "We'll thrash this out." THRASH it out Informal — work disputes, negotiations
talk shop talk shop (no object) · "Stop talking shop!" talk SHOP Informal — used when criticising the behaviour
clam up clam up (about sth) · often + "around sb" CLAM up Informal — emotional / personal situations

broach the subject broach the subject / topic (of sth) · "How do I broach it?" BROACH the SUB·ject Neutral — any sensitive / difficult topic

go on about go on (and on) about sth · "Stop going on about it!" go ON a·bout Informal — often used to express mild irritation

Listening: Conversations in Action

📋 Before this stage

This stage uses audio from the textbook — Tracks 2.11 and 2.12 (page 21). Prepare access to the audio before the lesson. The exercises below follow CELTA's listening framework: predict → gist → detail → produce.

CELTA — Pre-listening: Prediction & Activation
Step 1 — Before you listen
Discuss these two questions to activate background knowledge and set up a purpose for listening.
1
What kinds of problems can come up when you're trying to hold your own in a fast, group conversation in English?
2
How do people end a conversation naturally in English — without it feeling abrupt? What phrases or strategies do you already know?
CELTA — While Listening: Gist
Step 2 — Listen for gist (Track 2.11)
🎧 Track 2.11

Listen once without stopping. Answer these questions from memory:

  • Who are the people in the conversation, and what is the setting?
  • What is the general mood — relaxed, awkward, tense?
  • What is the main topic they keep coming back to?

🔊 Technique focus

Listen again. Which phrases from Useful Phrases 1 (Stage 6 — keeping the conversation going) do you recognise? Note them down. How many from each category do you catch?

CELTA — While Listening: Specific Information
Step 3 — Listen for detail (Track 2.12)
🎧 Track 2.12

This track focuses on ending a conversation. Listen and note:

  • Write down every phrase used to signal the end of the conversation.
  • Who initiates the ending — one person, or both?
  • What technique makes the exit feel natural rather than abrupt?
  • How does the intonation change on the final phrases?

📝 Check against Stage 6

Compare your notes with the gap-fill answers from Useful Phrases 2 (Stage 6, Exercise 2). How many did you catch? Which ones were harder to hear — and why? (fast speech, weak forms, linking, etc.)

CELTA — Post-listening: Production
Step 4 — Now you try it
Your teacher sets the scene. Improvise the end of the conversation using at least two phrases from Useful Phrases 2 and one vocabulary item from today's lesson. Try to sound natural — not scripted.
A
You've bumped into a neighbour at the supermarket. It's a warm chat — but you're in a rush and need to leave.
B
You're at a work social and you've been talking to a colleague for a while. You'd like to mingle, but don't want to be rude.
C
A friend calls you for a catch-up. You're exhausted and need to end the call — without sounding dismissive.
D
You're at a dinner party and it's getting late. You've been in a great conversation — time to wrap it up gracefully.

🎯 Reflection

After the role plays: which ending phrases felt most natural to you? Which felt awkward or hard to produce spontaneously? Set yourself a challenge: use one of the phrases in a real conversation before the next lesson, and report back.
