True or False? Heist Edition
Can you tell fact from fiction? These are all real — or are they?

1. In 1990, thirteen masterpieces worth over $500 million were stolen from a museum in Boston — and have never been found.

2. The gang behind the 2015 Hatton Garden vault robbery in London were young criminals in their 20s — chosen for their agility.

3. Before it was stolen in 1911, the Mona Lisa was a relatively obscure painting. The theft — and the media frenzy that followed — turned it into the world's most famous artwork.

4. In 2005, thieves in Brazil spent months posing as a fake landscaping company. They were actually digging a tunnel into a bank vault — and stole approximately $70 million in banknotes.

5. Pablo Picasso was questioned by French police as a suspect in the 1911 theft of the Mona Lisa.

6. Most home burglaries happen at night or on weekends, when it is easier to go unnoticed.

7. In the 2003 Antwerp Diamond Heist — the largest diamond robbery in history — all of the thieves were caught and most of the diamonds were recovered.

1 → True. The Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum heist remains the largest unsolved art theft in history.
2 → False. The Hatton Garden robbers averaged over 60 years old — the press called them the "Bad Grandpas."
3 → True. The Mona Lisa theft sparked global headlines and enormous public interest for the first time.
4 → True. The Banco Central heist in Fortaleza, Brazil is one of the largest cash robberies in history.
5 → True. Picasso had previously bought stolen goods from the same dealer and was brought in for questioning.
6 → False. Studies show that most burglaries happen during the day on weekdays — when people are out at work.
7 → False. Only four thieves were arrested, and most of the diamonds — estimated at over $100 million — were never recovered.

The $100 Million Sandwich
Antwerp, Belgium — February 2003

On a cold February weekend, a gang of thieves pulled off what many called the "heist of the century." They had spent months preparing. One gang member had even rented an office inside the Antwerp Diamond Centre — one of the most secure buildings in the world — to study the security system from the inside.

Over the weekend, they drilled through concrete, disabled the alarms, and broke into 123 out of 160 safe deposit boxes. By Sunday evening, they had stolen diamonds, gold, and jewellery worth an estimated $100 million (some say far more).

But there was one small problem.

On their way out of the city, the gang stopped in a forest to divide the loot and rest. When they left, they took the diamonds — but forgot the rubbish. Police found their campsite. Among the litter was a partially eaten salami sandwich.

The DNA from that sandwich led directly to Leonardo Notarbartolo — the man who had rented the office. He was arrested, tried, and sentenced to ten years in prison.

The diamonds? Most were never recovered.

💡 Moral of the story: you can plan the perfect crime — but never underestimate a sandwich.
