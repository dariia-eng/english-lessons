What Does It Mean to Truly Flourish?

The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle believed that happiness was not something that happened to you — it was something you achieved. He called this highest form of human wellbeing eudaimonia (from eu, "good", and daimon, "spirit" or "inner self"). For Aristotle, eudaimonia was not the giddy pleasure of a night out or the transient satisfaction of a new purchase. It was the deep, enduring contentment that comes from living in accordance with your highest nature — striving toward meaningful goals, developing your character, and contributing to something greater than yourself.

Twenty-three centuries later, psychologists are reaching strikingly similar conclusions.

🔬 Research spotlight: The PERMA Model (Seligman, 2011)
Positive psychologist Martin Seligman proposed five elements of genuine wellbeing: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Achievement. What is striking is that only one of these — positive emotion — corresponds to simple pleasure. The others point directly toward what Aristotle meant by eudaimonia: deep engagement with meaningful work, authentic human connection, and a sense of purpose that transcends the self.

Perhaps the most compelling evidence comes from Harvard's Study of Adult Development — the longest-running study of adult life ever conducted. Beginning in 1938, researchers tracked over 700 men across their entire lifetimes, measuring everything from career success to physical health. Their conclusion, published after more than 80 years of data collection, was unambiguous: the quality of your relationships is the single most powerful predictor of late-life happiness and health. Not wealth, not fame, not professional achievement — but the depth and warmth of human connection.

"The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80." — Robert Waldinger, Director, Harvard Study of Adult Development

Equally influential is the work of psychologists Richard Ryan and Edward Deci on Self-Determination Theory. Their research shows that human beings have three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy (feeling in control of your own choices), competence (feeling genuinely capable), and relatedness (feeling meaningfully connected to others). When these needs are met, people flourish. When they are frustrated — when we work purely for external rewards, or feel controlled and isolated — motivation collapses and wellbeing suffers.

Both of these frameworks support Aristotle's crucial distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Research by psychologist Tim Kasser found that people who strongly prioritise extrinsic goals — money, status, appearance — actually report lower life satisfaction than those who do not, even when they successfully achieve those goals. Meanwhile, those who cultivate intrinsic goals — learning, creativity, meaningful relationships — consistently report greater wellbeing across cultures.

So why do so many of us still chase the extrinsic? Perhaps because intrinsic rewards are harder to see and slower to arrive. A pay rise shows up in your bank account immediately. A deepened friendship, a developed skill, or a life of quiet virtue — these take years to build, and their value is invisible to the outside world. In a culture that constantly measures success in numbers, the flourishing life demands something unusual: the courage to pursue what is meaningful rather than merely measurable.

Key Vocabulary

Each word appeared in the reading. Study the definition, the real quote using it, and the grammar note. Try completing each "Use it" sentence aloud with your teacher.
transient
adjective
/ˈtrænziənt/
Lasting only a short time; not permanent. Opposite: enduring, lasting, permanent. Formal register — common in academic and philosophical writing.
"The pursuit of pleasure produces only transient satisfaction — the joy of a new car fades within weeks as we adapt to our new circumstances." — Dan Gilbert, Stumbling on Happiness
"Social media offers _______ rewards — the dopamine hit from a 'like' disappears almost immediately."
enduring
adjective
/ɪnˈdjʊərɪŋ/
Lasting for a long time; continuing to exist. Also a verb: to endure = to suffer through something. Collocates with: legacy, appeal, love, impact, friendship.
"Aristotle's most enduring contribution may be the idea that the good life is not about what you feel, but about who you become." — Edith Hall, Aristotle's Way
"The Harvard study's most _______ finding is that relationships, not wealth, predict late-life happiness."
transcend
verb
/trænˈsend/
To go beyond the usual limits of something. Formal. Common phrases: transcend differences / the self / time / culture.
"The need for meaning — for a life that transcends our own petty concerns — may be the most distinctively human of all our needs." — Viktor Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning
"Seligman argues that genuine wellbeing must _______ positive emotion alone."
compelling
adjective
/kəmˈpelɪŋ/
So convincing or interesting it's hard to ignore. Common collocations: compelling evidence / argument / case / story. Verb form: compel.
"The compelling thing about the Harvard data is not what it says about wealth or fame — it is what it says about love." — Robert Waldinger, TED Talk (2015)
"What is most _______ about Self-Determination Theory is how universal its findings appear across different cultures."
cultivate
verb
/ˈkʌltɪveɪt/
To develop a skill, quality or relationship carefully over time. Originally agricultural. Metaphorical: cultivate a skill / friendship / habit / mindset / patience. Implies deliberate, gradual effort.
"Happiness is not something ready-made. It comes from your own actions. You have to actively cultivate it — day by day, choice by choice." — Dalai Lama XIV
"People who _______ deep friendships throughout their lives tend to be far healthier in old age."
prioritise
verb
/praɪˈɒrɪtaɪz/
To treat something as more important than other things. Noun forms: priority, priorities. Pattern: prioritise X over Y. Useful in arguments: "Society should prioritise wellbeing over GDP."
"When people prioritise extrinsic goals — money, status, image — above all else, they tend to report lower wellbeing even after achieving them." — Tim Kasser, The High Price of Materialism
"Modern schools often _______ academic achievement over students' emotional and social development."

Who Lives Happier?

Meet three people. Read their profiles, then discuss: who do you think lives the happiest life, and why? Use vocabulary from the text and refer to Aristotle's ideas in your answer.
Marco, 34
Social media influencer · Milan
Hedonic
Marco has 2 million followers and earns well from brand deals. He travels constantly, attends parties, and always has the latest gadgets. His life looks glamorous online — but he works 14-hour days chasing trends, rarely sleeps, and struggles to maintain close friendships. He feels anxious whenever engagement drops.
Elena, 41
Primary school teacher · Kyiv
Eudaimonic
Elena earns a modest salary, but finds deep meaning in her work. She has a tight-knit group of friends, volunteers at weekends, and is learning to paint. She says some weeks are exhausting, but she almost always goes to bed feeling that her day had a point. She rarely thinks about money or status.
James, 52
Investment banker · London
Mixed
James is extremely successful by conventional measures — a large house, private school for his kids, holidays in the Maldives. He enjoys his work but admits it leaves little room for anything else. He has acquaintances rather than friends, and has begun questioning whether he made the right choices. He plans to "start living" when he retires.
