Part B — Reading: Famous Financial Bubbles
📖 Read the texts about three famous financial bubbles
A financial bubble occurs when the price of an asset rises far above its real value, driven by speculation and over-optimism rather than genuine demand. When confidence suddenly disappears, prices collapse — often taking investors' savings with them. Read about three examples from modern history.

🛢️ Text A — The Oil Bubble (2007–2008)

In the early 2000s, the global demand for oil was growing rapidly, particularly in emerging economies such as China and India. Oil companies and investors became increasingly optimistic, and the price per barrel began to rise steadily. By July 2008, oil had reached a record high of nearly $147 per barrel — more than five times its price just a few years earlier. Speculators were borrowing money to buy oil futures, convinced that prices would keep rising indefinitely.

However, the bubble burst in dramatic fashion. As the global financial crisis took hold in late 2008, factories closed, flights were cancelled, and people drove their cars far less. Demand collapsed almost overnight. By December 2008, the price had fallen to around $32 per barrel — a drop of nearly 80%. Many investors who had borrowed heavily to speculate on oil prices faced ruin. Oil-producing nations such as Russia and Venezuela, which had based their entire budgets on high oil prices, were forced to make severe spending cuts.

🏠 Text B — The US Housing Bubble (2004–2008)

In the early 2000s, banks and mortgage companies in the United States began offering home loans to borrowers who had poor credit histories or very low incomes. These loans — known as "subprime mortgages" — were based on the assumption that house prices would always rise. Estate agents, banks, and homeowners all shared the same optimistic belief: property was a safe investment, and prices could only go up.

The number of people buying homes with borrowed money increased dramatically, pushing prices to record levels. However, by 2006 the market began to slow. When interest rates rose, many borrowers could no longer afford their monthly repayments. Houses were repossessed on a massive scale, banks found themselves holding billions of dollars of worthless debt, and financial panic spread worldwide. The result was the worst global recession since the Great Depression of the 1930s. Millions of Americans lost their homes, their jobs, and their savings — the consequences of believing that a bubble could never burst.

₿ Text C — The Bitcoin Bubble (2017)

Bitcoin, the world's first and most famous cryptocurrency, had existed since 2009 but remained relatively obscure until 2017. That year, its price rose at a breathtaking speed. At the start of 2017, one Bitcoin was worth around $1,000. By mid-December, the price had soared to nearly $20,000 — a rise of roughly 2,000% in a single year. Media coverage was intense, and thousands of ordinary people invested their savings, driven by the fear of missing out on what seemed like guaranteed wealth.

Then, almost immediately after reaching its peak, the price began to fall. Within a year, Bitcoin had lost around 80% of its value, dropping below $3,500. Many small investors who had bought at or near the top lost the majority of their money. Unlike tulip bulbs or oil barrels, however, the story did not end there. Bitcoin later recovered and, at times, exceeded its 2017 high — suggesting that while speculation played a large role, the underlying technology may have genuine long-term value.
