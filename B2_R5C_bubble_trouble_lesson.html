Why did tulips become so valuable in the 17th century?

💡 Show answer / key ideas▶

Tulips were beautiful and rare — they had only recently arrived from the Ottoman Empire. Wealthy Dutch merchants wanted beautiful things, so demand rose rapidly. The more beautiful and unusual the tulip, the more expensive it became. People began borrowing money to buy and sell bulbs, speculating that prices would keep rising.

Adverb + adjective collocations

To make any adjective stronger you can use the adverb really but other adverbs are also common and certain adverbs frequently collocate with certain adjectives. For example, it's much more common to say absolutely ridiculous than highly ridiculous. Highly ridiculous is not wrong but it's not common. Here are some common adverb + adjective collocations:

absolutelyright, true, essential,
bitterlycold, divided, opposed,
completelydifferent, wrong, honest,
deeplyreligious, personal, concerned
entirelydifferent, new, possible
extremelydifficult, important, dangerous,
highlyunlikely, qualified, effective,
perfectlygood, safe, legal,
relativelysmall, new, simple,
totallyunacceptable, lost, wrong
widelyknown, accepted, used,

Student A — Story · Page 170
💻 The Dot-com Bubble
USA · 1995–2002

In the mid-1990s, the internet was entirely new and people were deeply convinced it would change the world. They were right — but they were also completely wrong about how quickly it would make money.

Investors poured money into any company with ".com" in its name. It didn't matter whether the company made a profit — or even had a realistic plan for making one. Stock prices became wildly overvalued. Pets.com, an online pet supply store, raised $82 million in an IPO; it went bankrupt just nine months later. Webvan, an online grocery delivery service, burned through over a billion dollars before collapsing. A remarkably large number of these companies had never made a single dollar of profit.

At the peak in March 2000, the NASDAQ technology index had risen by over 400% in just five years. Investors were absolutely certain prices would keep rising. Then, almost overnight, confidence collapsed. By 2002, the NASDAQ had fallen by 78%, wiping out roughly $5 trillion in value. Hundreds of dot-com companies went bankrupt. Thousands of people lost their jobs, and many ordinary investors lost their savings — they were bitterly disappointed and deeply in debt.

Interestingly, the damage was relatively limited in the long run. Companies that survived — like Amazon and Google — became the most valuable in history. But for those who had invested at the peak, the crash was extremely painful. The dot-com bubble remains one of the most widely known examples of speculative excess in modern history.

Student B — Story · Page 174
🏠 The US Housing Bubble
USA · 2001–2008

In the early 2000s, buying a house in the United States seemed like the perfectly safe investment. House prices had risen consistently for decades. Banks were highly motivated to lend money — and they began offering mortgages to people who were barely qualified to repay them. These were called "subprime" mortgages.

The logic seemed relatively simple: if a borrower couldn't repay, the bank could simply sell the house — because prices would always rise. Banks packaged these risky loans together and sold them to investors around the world, who were deeply convinced they were buying a perfectly safe asset. A huge number of people bought second or third homes purely as investments, completely certain they could sell at a profit.

By 2006, US house prices had risen by over 120% in a decade. Then, in 2007, a large number of subprime borrowers began defaulting on their loans. House prices — which had seemed entirely stable — collapsed almost overnight. Major banks that had bought the packaged mortgages suddenly found themselves heavily indebted. Lehman Brothers, one of the world's biggest investment banks, went bankrupt in September 2008.

The consequences were extremely serious — not just for the USA but for the entire global economy. Millions of Americans lost their homes. Stock markets around the world crashed. Governments spent trillions of dollars bailing out banks. The recession that followed was the worst since the 1930s. Those who had been bitterly opposed to the risky lending practices felt absolutely vindicated — but the damage had already been done.

💡 What did both bubbles have in common?▶
  • Prices became wildly overvalued — disconnected from real worth
  • People were absolutely certain prices would keep rising
  • A relatively small number of early investors made fortunes
  • The majority of ordinary investors were bitterly disappointed

  • Dot-com: mainly affected tech investors · Housing: affected the entire global economy
  • Dot-com: some companies (Amazon, Google) survived and became highly successful · Housing: relatively few positive long-term outcomes
