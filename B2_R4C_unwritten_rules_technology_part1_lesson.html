Reading: Back to the World
Read, understand, and discuss
Before you read: It's 2026. Imagine Frank has just been released after 14 years with zero access to technology. Look at the list below — which three would shock him the most, and why? Discuss with a partner before reading.
🤖 An AI copilot writes your emails for you 🎭 Deepfakes — fake videos of real people saying things they never said 📱 People doomscroll for hours without noticing 🌀 The algorithm decides what you see, buy, and think 👻 People ghost friends — just stop replying with no explanation 🧠 Short-form content has replaced books, long films, and conversations

In those first few days of freedom, I did all the things I'd dreamt about for nearly fourteen years: I ate out, I slept in, I met up with people and I walked and walked in the spring sunshine. It was glorious but deeply confusing – so much had changed. People said things that I simply didn't understand: 'Just prompt it', 'The algorithm will find something' or 'Are you even AI-literate?' When I looked blank, they'd shake their heads and move on.

Fourteen years ago, people had used phones for talking. Now they barely looked up at all. They scrolled endlessly — doomscrolling, someone called it — and talked to invisible assistants instead of each other. I watched a man ghost his friend in real time: a conversation that just… stopped. No explanation. And everywhere I went, screens showed me faces of people saying things I was told they had never actually said. Deepfakes, they called them. I didn't know what was real.

What I found hardest was the speed of it all. Everything was short — short videos, short messages, short attention spans. People were always-on but somehow never present. I had been away for fourteen years, but standing there among them, I felt like I had arrived from a different planet entirely.

— Frank

📝 Comprehension — click each question to reveal a model answer:

1. What did Frank find confusing when he came back to the world? ▼
He found almost everything confusing. Language had changed — people used phrases like "just prompt it" or "are you AI-literate?" that meant nothing to him. Behaviour had changed too: people doomscrolled instead of talking, and ghosted friends without a word. He also couldn't trust what he saw on screen, because deepfakes made videos of real people saying things they had never said.
2. What specific language did Frank hear that he didn't understand? ▼
He heard phrases like "just prompt it", "the algorithm will find something", and "are you even AI-literate?" These expressions come from a world built around AI tools that didn't exist when Frank went to prison.
3. How had the way people communicate changed? ▼
People no longer really talked to each other. Instead, they doomscrolled through screens, asked AI assistants questions, and ghosted people — ending friendships by simply going silent. Frank noticed they were always-on but somehow never fully present.
4. What did Frank mean when he said he "didn't know what was real"? ▼
He was referring to deepfakes — AI-generated videos showing real people saying things they had never actually said. In 2026, it is no longer possible to trust a video just because you can see someone's face. For Frank, coming from a world with no such technology, this was particularly disorienting.

💬 Discuss:

  • ▸ Frank was confused by phrases like "just prompt it" and "are you AI-literate?" Which three phrases from 2026 do you think would shock him most — and why?
  • ▸ Frank noticed people were always-on but never present. Do you recognise that feeling in your own life?
  • ▸ Frank couldn't tell what was real because of deepfakes. Can you? How do you decide whether a video or image is genuine in 2026?
  • ▸ What aspect of digital life today do you think we'll look back on as strange — or even embarrassing — in ten years?

What Would You Tell Frank?
Read the advice — then rate it and debate it
Frank's family and friends offered four different pieces of advice. Read them all, then answer the questions below.
Mo says:
"The first thing you need to do is become AI-literate — learn what AI tools can do, and how people use them every day. Most people now use an AI copilot to write emails, plan tasks, or search for information. Start there. But be careful: online you'll find a lot of AI slop — content that looks real but is empty and generated by a machine. Not everything you read is written by a human."

Does this advice make sense for Frank?

Ali says:
"Before you trust anyone online, prompt an AI copilot to help you check who they are — look at their digital footprint, their posts, their history. And remember: even a video of someone's face saying something might be a deepfake. Don't believe everything you see. If someone makes you uncomfortable, it's completely normal to ghost them — just stop responding. You don't owe anyone an explanation online."

Does this advice make sense for Frank?

Lisa says:
"The hardest thing to understand is the algorithm. It decides what you see, what you read, and even what you think you want — and it is designed to keep you scrolling. Most people now consume short-form content instead of reading properly, and it is doing real damage to concentration. Researchers are calling it brain rot. Stay curious, read long things, and don't let the algorithm decide who you are."

Does this advice make sense for Frank?

Collette says:
"Whatever you do, don't let yourself become always-on. Everyone around you will seem chronically online — checking their phones constantly, doomscrolling at dinner, replying to work messages at midnight. You don't have to live like that. Do a digital detox one day a week. Meet people face to face. You spent fourteen years without a screen — that is actually your advantage, not your problem."

Does this advice make sense for Frank?

🎯 Discussion questions:

  1. 1. Mo says Frank needs to become AI-literate. What does that mean in practice? What would you teach him first?
  2. 2. Ali says it's "completely normal" to ghost someone online. Do you agree? Is ghosting ever acceptable — or always rude?
  3. 3. Lisa warns Frank about brain rot from short-form content and the algorithm. Do you feel the algorithm controls what you think — or do you stay in control?
  4. 4. Collette says Frank's fourteen years without screens is "an advantage, not a problem." What does she mean? Do you think she's right?
  5. 5. Whose advice do you think is the most important for Frank — and the most important for you personally? Are they the same person?
  6. 6. Write one extra sentence of advice for Frank that none of the four people gave. Use at least one word from today's vocabulary.
