Context📋 Seven Tips for More Effective Meetings

Read this extract from a business communication guide. The words in teal are today's target vocabulary.

📖 Source: Adapted from The Art of the Meeting, a guide for managers and team leads in international workplaces.
1
Avoid spending a disproportionate amount of time on a single item. If a discussion is going in circles, it's time to move on.
2
Ensure you gather input from everyone in the room before reaching a consensus. Dominant voices can silence quieter perspectives.
3
When the group gets stuck, the chair should intervene: "Let's move on and come back to this later." Parking an issue is not abandoning it.
4
A well-structured agenda, circulated in advance, is the single most effective tool for keeping meetings focused and time-efficient.
5
When a participant goes off topic, it's the chair's responsibility to redirect them diplomatically — not to ignore them. Saying "That's an interesting point — could we add it to the agenda for next time?" is far more effective.
6
Brief small talk before a meeting can strengthen team dynamics, but know when to draw the line. A clear signal like "Shall we get down to business?" helps everyone shift focus.
7
If you consistently fail to get through your full agenda, the problem isn't time — it's planning. Prioritise items and be realistic about what's achievable in the time available.

Study the reference cards — pay attention to grammar patterns and common mistakes. Then complete the exercise below.

noun (countable)
an agenda
a structured list of topics to be discussed in a meeting
"Could you circulate the agenda before Thursday?"
⚠️ Always: an agenda (not "a agenda")
noun (countable)
an item
a single point or topic listed on an agenda
"The third item on the agenda is the rebranding proposal."
⚠️ item ≠ agenda — an agenda contains multiple items
noun (uncountable)
input
ideas, opinions or contributions someone makes on a topic
"I'd appreciate your input on this proposal."
⚠️ No plural: not "inputs" in this context
phrasal verb
move on (to)
to stop discussing the current topic and proceed to the next
"Right, let's move on to the next item."
⚠️ Not "move" alone — the particle "on" is essential
phrasal verb
come back to
to return to a topic or issue at a later point
"Let's park this and come back to it after lunch."
⚠️ "come back to" — not "come back at"
verb phrase
stay on topic / go off topic
remain focused on / deviate from the agreed discussion points
"I know this is interesting, but let's try to stay on topic."
⚠️ No article: "off topic" — not "off the topic"
noun (uncountable)
small talk
light, social conversation on unimportant topics, typically before a meeting
"There was five minutes of small talk before we got started."
⚠️ No article: "make small talk" — not "make a small talk"
verb phrase
get down to business
to start the main, important part of the discussion
"Shall we get down to business?"
Fixed phrase — don't change "business" to another word
phrasal verb
get through
to successfully complete or deal with a set of tasks or topics
"We only got through half the agenda."
Implies effort — completing everything wasn't automatic

Pronunciation🔊 Fluency in context

At B2+, it's not enough to say phrases correctly — you need to sound natural and confident in meetings. Focus on linking, weak forms, and chunking.

🔊 Connected speech & chunking

/ˈmuːv ɒn/
"Let's move on to the next item."
Two content words stressed: MOVE and ITEM. "Let's" /lets/, "to the" → /tə ðə/. Practice as one breath group.
/ɡɛt daʊn tə ˈbɪznəs/
"Shall we get down to business?"
"to" → /tə/ (weak). BUsiness = 2 syllables /ˈbɪz.nəs/ — NOT three. Native speakers link "get_down" smoothly.
/kəm ˈbæk tuː ɪt/
"We can always come back to it."
When "to" is followed by a vowel it's strong /tuː/. "come" is often reduced to /kəm/. The "t" in "it" is often a flap: /ɪɾ/.
/ˌsmɔːl ˈtɔːk/
"a bit of small talk"
Compound noun — both words stressed, TALK slightly more. Often preceded by "a bit of" in natural speech: /ə bɪt əv/.
/ɡɛt θruː/
"Did we get through everything?"
The /θ/ in "through" is a common L1 difficulty — don't replace with /f/ or /t/. "get" is often reduced to /ɡət/ in mid-sentence.
