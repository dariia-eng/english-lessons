Lead-in
Brainstorming techniques
Exercise 1 — Look at the pictures (A–D)
What can you see in each picture? What do you think each technique involves? Tell your teacher.
ARound Robin
Each person contributes one idea at a time in turn, going around the group until ideas are exhausted.
BRole Storming
You brainstorm as if you were a different person — a boss, customer, or industry expert.
CQuiet Brainstorming
Everyone writes ideas silently and simultaneously on sticky notes — no talking until the end.
DReverse Brainstorming
Instead of “How do we solve this?” you ask “How could we cause this problem?” then reverse the answers.
Exercise 1 — Discussion

2Have you ever used any of these techniques — or something similar? What was it like?
3Which technique would you find easiest to use? Which would you find most challenging, and why?

Language Focus
Question forms — 6 types
Question Forms — Reference
1. Negative questions
“Isn’t brainstorming basically just people thinking of ideas together?”
2. Statement + question word (echo)
“You call it what?”
3. You mean… / So you’re saying…
“You mean you ask people to think of how to cause a problem?”
4. Indirect questions
“I wonder if you could tell our listeners what they should do…”
5. Question tags
“You’re going to talk to us about brainstorming today, aren’t you?”
6. Cleft questions
“What exactly is it that makes them so loyal?”
How to form each question type
Formula → structure → examples from the lesson
5. Question tag — confirm or check what you think is true
Positive statement + auxn’t + pronoun?
Negative statement + aux + pronoun?
“You’re going to talk about brainstorming today, aren’t you?”
“You don’t work alone, do you?”
⚠️ Positive statement → negative tag  |  Negative statement → positive tag
1. Negative question — express surprise, challenge an assumption
Auxn’t + subject + verb phrase?
“Isn’t brainstorming basically just people thinking of ideas together?”
“Didn’t you say there were four different types?”
⚠️ Word order: aux comes first, before the subject (same as a regular question)
2. Echo question — show surprise, ask to repeat or confirm
Type A: Statement + wh-word?
Type B: Surprising word/phrase + rising intonation
“You call it what?”  (Type A)
A: “I use Role Storming.”  →  B: “Role Storming?”  (Type B)
A: “She invented it in 1953.”  →  B: “She did?”  (Type B)
3. You mean… — paraphrase to check understanding
You mean / So you’re saying + subject + verb (normal order)?
“You mean you ask people to think of how to cause a problem?”
“So you’re saying anyone can lead a Round Robin?”
⚠️ No inversion after you mean — keep normal subject + verb order
4. Indirect question — polite, embedded, formal
Intro phrase + if / wh-word + subject + verb (no inversion)
“I wonder if you could tell our listeners what they should do?”
“Could you explain what the difference is?”
Intro phrases: I wonder if… / Could you tell me… / Do you know… / I’d like to know…
⚠️ No inversion: “I wonder is it / what it is”
6. Cleft question — emphasise a specific element
What + is it + that + subject + verb?
“What is it that makes these techniques so effective?”
“What exactly is it that companies get wrong about brainstorming?”
Variation: What + [noun phrase] + is it that…?  →  “What advice is it that you would give?”

Speaking 3
Situations & critical thinking
Exercise 9 — When does each technique fail?
Every technique has limits. For each condition below, discuss: which technique is most affected? How would you adapt it or work around the problem?
⚠️ Strict hierarchy: junior employees won't challenge their manager out loud — even in a Round Robin.
⚠️ Cultural discomfort: people feel awkward or embarrassed pretending to be someone else — Role Storming feels forced.
⚠️ Vague problem: Reverse Brainstorming requires a clearly defined challenge — a fuzzy goal produces useless flips.
⚠️ No follow-through: Quiet Brainstorming creates dozens of sticky notes that nobody reads or discusses — ideas die on the wall.
⚠️ Time pressure: the team has 10 minutes, not 45 — which technique still works under a tight deadline?
Follow-up: Have you been in any of these situations? What actually happened?
Exercise 10 — Design the session
You are a creativity consultant. Your client describes a problem. Recommend a technique (or a combination), explain why, and predict at least one obstacle.
🏥 Client A. A hospital needs to cut patient waiting times. Senior doctors get defensive when efficiency is questioned.
🎓 Client B. A university group wants research ideas but the team is shy — meetings feel awkward and unproductive.
🛒 Client C. A retail chain is losing customers. Leadership keeps proposing price cuts; the marketing team suspects the real issue is brand perception.
🎮 Client D. A games studio has been debating the same two design options for weeks — the team is stuck and losing motivation.
Structure your recommendation:
I’d suggest [technique] because…  ·  The key advantage here is…  ·  One risk is that…, but you could address it by…  ·  You might also combine it with…
Exercise 11 — Head to head
Compare the two techniques in each pair. For each context, which would you choose and why? Can you think of a situation where the other one would be better?
Pair 1 — Generating volume
🔵 Round Robin vs 🟡 Quiet Brainstorming — Your goal: generate as many ideas as possible in 20 minutes with a team of 12.
Pair 2 — Diagnosing failure
🟠 Role Storming vs 🔴 Reverse Brainstorming — Your goal: understand why a new product flopped with customers.
Pair 3 — Remote team
🔵 Round Robin vs 🔴 Reverse Brainstorming — Your goal: run a productive online brainstorm across 4 time zones, all in one call.
Useful language: The main advantage of X over Y in this case is…  ·  Y would fall down here because…  ·  Having said that, there’s a strong argument for…
Exercise 12 — Devil’s advocate
Take turns making a strong case against brainstorming techniques. The other person defends them. Swap after 2 minutes. Use at least two question forms from Stage 3.
🗡️ “Isn’t it true that most brainstorming sessions just produce obvious ideas that nobody acts on?”
🗡️ “What is it about brainstorming that makes introverts feel excluded rather than included?”
🗡️ “Couldn’t companies save hours of time just by asking people to email their ideas instead?”
🗡️ “You don’t actually believe Reverse Brainstorming makes people think differently, do you?”
Defending: That’s a fair point, but…  ·  I’d push back on that — isn’t it the case that…?  ·  What I’d say is that the real issue is…
