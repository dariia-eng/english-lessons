Activity 4
The Candle Problem
Look at the items below, read the task carefully, and discuss how you would solve it with a partner. Do not read ahead! 👥 Partner
You have exactly three items:
🕯️
a small candle
📦
a box of drawing pins
🔥
a book of matches
Your task: Attach the candle to the wall so that, when lit, it does not drip wax onto the floor below it. You may only use the three items shown above — nothing else.

💬 Discuss possible solutions with your partner. What would you try first?
The solution: Empty the drawing pins from their box. Pin the box itself flat against the wall — it acts as a small shelf or platform. Place the candle inside the box and light it. The box catches any dripping wax.

💡 Most people fail because they see the box only as a container for pins, not as a tool in itself. This mental block is called functional fixedness — and it is exactly what the next article explores.
Think about it
Which type of thinking is most useful here — lateral, logical, wishful, or critical? Can you explain why?

The Candle Problem
Read the text. For gaps (1–8), consider what type of word might fit before you answer the questions on the next page.
German psychologist Karl Duncker's 'Candle Problem' experiment (0)sets out one way in which our minds approach tasks differently. Duncker's proposition was that, by (1)………………… the power of creative thought, we can look at a familiar object and consider how it might otherwise be used in order to serve a new purpose. This test is (2)………………… used by psychologists to measure the personality trait of 'functional fixedness', a type of thinking that prevents people from using their creativity to (3)………………… problems.
Those who fail to provide the correct answer to the Candle Problem usually (4)………………… this trait, and they are not alone. In fact, the vast majority of people see an object purely for its (5)………………… use, and rarely stop to consider an alternative, even a purpose for which it might (6)………………… highly suitable. However, those who do succeed have the (7)………………… to be creative and flexible thinkers who are capable of avoiding functional fixedness, and do not naturally conform to (8)…………………, particularly when a problem requires them to think 'outside-of-the-box'.
Which types of thinking do people use when trying to solve the Candle Problem? Write your answer below.
Suggested answer: People who fail the Candle Problem rely on logical thinking — they see the box purely as a container and apply its obvious function. Those who succeed use lateral thinking: they break free from functional fixedness and see the box as a potential shelf. Critical thinking also plays a role in evaluating possible solutions and rejecting the obvious ones.

Key language from this unit
functional fixedness — the personality trait that prevents people from using creativity to address problems
employing (the power of) — making use of something as a tool or means; collocates naturally with power, skill, method
commonly used — the standard adverb collocation for widespread professional practice; not authentically or entirely
address problems — fixed academic collocation: to deal with or tackle; not determine, reach, or contend
display a trait — to show or exhibit a personality characteristic; the natural collocation with traits and behaviours
given use — established or designated purpose (fixed adjective phrase)
prove + adjective — link verb meaning "turn out to be": prove highly suitable, prove difficult, prove useful
have a tendency to — natural inclination or disposition; fixed collocation structure: have the tendency to do sth
conform to convention — fixed collocation: to follow accepted norms; not assumption, opinion, or prediction
