Would You Eat It?

Five unusual foods from around the world · B1 · ~300 words

Every culture has foods that seem completely normal to locals — but absolutely shocking to visitors. Here are five dishes that might make you think twice… or maybe book a flight.

🍈
🇲🇾Durian
Southeast Asia — Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia
ADurian is a large, spiky fruit often called the "king of fruits." Inside, its flesh is soft, rich, and creamy — and people who love it say nothing else tastes quite like it. There is just one problem: the smell. Many describe it as a mix of old socks, onions, and rubbish. Because of this, durian is banned in hotels, airports, and public transport across much of Southeast Asia.

BDespite the famous smell, millions of people eat durian every day and cannot understand why anyone would refuse it. In Malaysia and Thailand, durian season is a major event. Families drive to farms, pay a lot of money for the best varieties, and eat it together outside — because no one is allowed to bring it indoors.
🌡️ Dare rating: 6/10 — the smell is the real challenge
🐟
🇮🇸Hákarl
Iceland
CHákarl is fermented Greenlandic shark — one of the most extreme foods in the world. The shark is buried underground for six to twelve weeks, then hung up to dry for several months. The process is so long because fresh Greenlandic shark is actually poisonous. Only after months of preparation is it safe to eat.

DThe smell is so powerful that first-timers often gag before they even taste it. The celebrity chef Anthony Bourdain once called it "the single worst thing I have ever eaten." Even many Icelanders avoid it. But locals serve it at traditional festivals and consider it an important part of their cultural identity.
☠️ Dare rating: 9/10 — not for the faint-hearted
🥚
🇵🇭Balut
Philippines & Southeast Asia
EBalut is a fertilised duck egg that has been incubated for around 17 days. Inside the shell, you find a partially developed chick. Street vendors sell it at night markets, hot and ready to eat. You crack the shell, drink the broth, and eat the contents with a little salt and vinegar. For millions of Filipinos, it is a completely normal street snack — cheap, filling, and high in protein.

FFor visitors, the idea is harder to accept. But many who try it say it tastes like a very rich, flavourful egg. The key, people say, is not to think about it too much.
🤔 Dare rating: 8/10 — you have to commit
🕷️
🇰🇭Fried Tarantulas
Cambodia — town of Skuon
GIn the Cambodian town of Skuon, fried tarantulas are a popular street food. The spiders are seasoned with salt, sugar, and garlic, then deep-fried until crispy. Locals say they taste similar to soft-shell crab. The tradition began in the 1970s during a period of extreme food scarcity, when people had very little to eat and needed to find protein wherever they could.

HToday, fried tarantulas are popular with tourists as well as locals. Many visitors try one as a dare — and some say they are surprisingly good. Would you?
😱 Dare rating: 8/10 — crunchier than you think
🐜
🇲🇽Escamoles
Mexico
IEscamoles are the larvae of a type of ant, harvested from agave plant roots in central Mexico. They are fried in butter with onion and chilli, then served in tacos or omelettes. Despite their unusual origin, escamoles are a highly prized delicacy — sometimes called "insect caviar." They have a light, creamy, slightly nutty flavour that food lovers describe as surprisingly pleasant.

JA plate of escamoles in a high-end Mexico City restaurant can cost as much as a steak. The harvest season is short, the ants are hard to find, and removing the larvae without getting stung is difficult and time-consuming. That is why they are so expensive — and so special.
✨ Dare rating: 5/10 — fancy and actually delicious
